Trump Incarceration

As of — • Version 0

Status

Donald Trump has not been incarcerated. As of November 11, 2026, no criminal case against Trump has resulted in jail time. Trump was convicted of 34 felony counts in New York in May 2024 but received an unconditional discharge — no prison, no fines, no probation [s1]. All other criminal cases have been dismissed or dropped. The only live proceedings are two challenges to the New York conviction: a state appeal argued on November 5, 2026 and awaiting a decision [s14], and an appeal to the federal 2nd Circuit after a federal judge refused for the third time, on August 28, 2026, to move the case to federal court [s12][s13].

Active Cases

New York Hush Money Case — Conviction Under Appeal

On May 30, 2024, a Manhattan jury found Trump guilty of 34 felony counts of falsifying business records related to a $130,000 payment to adult film star Stormy Daniels during the 2016 presidential campaign [s1]. On January 10, 2025, Judge Juan Merchan sentenced Trump to an unconditional discharge, calling it the "only lawful sentence" to avoid "encroaching upon the highest office in the land" [s3]. The conviction stands on Trump's record, but carries no penalties.

Trump is appealing the conviction through multiple avenues:

State appeal: Trump's attorneys filed arguments in New York's Appellate Division, First Department claiming the trial was "fatally marred" by faulty evidence — including testimony related to official presidential acts that they argue is barred by the Supreme Court's 2024 immunity decision — and that Judge Merchan should have recused himself [s2].

A five-justice panel heard oral arguments on November 5, 2026. Trump lawyer Robert Giuffra told the court the case "was infected by multiple fatal errors under New York and federal law," arguing that prosecutors improperly relied on federal campaign finance law in a state case and that testimony from Hope Hicks, part of Michael Cohen's testimony, and Trump's tweets were shielded by presidential immunity. Steven Wu, for Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg, said federal law played a "minor role" and that the jury had "a menu of options." Justice Troy Webber questioned whether the campaign finance argument was enough to vacate a business records conviction. Justice Martin Shulman said the Hicks testimony "certainly seems to fit within the outer perimeters" of presidential immunity [s14]. The court did not rule from the bench, and no decision date has been announced.

Federal transfer bid: On February 4, 2026, U.S. District Judge Alvin Hellerstein heard nearly three hours of arguments on Trump's request to move the case from state to federal court, where he could seek dismissal on presidential immunity grounds. The 2nd U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals had directed Hellerstein in November 2025 to reconsider the matter after he previously denied it [s4][s5]. Hellerstein told Trump lawyer Jeffrey Wall: "You sought two bites at the apple," noting that Trump's team waited approximately 59 days after the Supreme Court's immunity ruling to seek federal removal, well past the customary 30-day window [s10].

On August 28, 2026, Hellerstein denied the request for the third time. He ruled that Trump waited too long and had no grounds to remove a case after conviction and sentencing: "He cannot play one court against another." He called Trump's reasons "neither new nor legally sufficient" and wrote that "paying hush money to an adult film star or engaging in a cover-up of an embarrassment are not subsumed in presidential immunity" [s12][s13]. Trump's lawyers called the ruling "baseless and lawless" and filed a notice of appeal to the 2nd Circuit the same day. No argument date has been reported [s13].

DOJ intervention: The U.S. Department of Justice — now led in part by Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, who served as Trump's defense attorney during the hush money trial — filed a proposed amicus brief supporting Trump's position in the state appeal. The brief cites the Supreme Court's 2024 presidential immunity decision, arguing that trial evidence related to official presidential acts should not have been admitted [s2][s11].

Path to Incarceration

There is currently no realistic near-term path to Trump's incarceration. The only remaining conviction (New York) already resulted in an unconditional discharge with no jail time. If the Appellate Division upholds the conviction, the sentence stays as it is, and the losing side can ask the New York Court of Appeals to review the case. If the conviction is overturned, Trump's record would be cleared unless prosecutors pursued a retrial. Neither outcome creates a likely route to jail [s3][s14].

For incarceration to become possible, one of these scenarios would need to occur:

New criminal charges: A new indictment — federal or state — would have to be brought while Trump is no longer protected by DOJ policy against prosecuting a sitting president. Current DOJ leadership has shown no indication of pursuing this. No state prosecution is pending or anticipated.

Post-presidency prosecution: The federal election interference case was dismissed "without prejudice," meaning charges could theoretically be refiled after Trump leaves office. However, political and institutional appetite for such a move is uncertain at best, and statutes of limitations may become a factor.

Conclusion: As of November 11, 2026, all criminal cases except the New York hush money conviction have been resolved without incarceration. Trump remains a convicted felon on paper but faces no imprisonment and no active criminal proceedings beyond the state and federal challenges to his existing conviction. The next signals to watch are the Appellate Division's decision and the 2nd Circuit's handling of the removal appeal.
